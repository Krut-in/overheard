Level 1 · 20 seconds

The pitch and why I built it

A station abroad talks at you all day, in a language you may not speak, and almost none of it is about you. Overheard knows your bookings. It listens to announcements and reads signs, boards and local news, then speaks only when something touches your trip. You hear it in your language, with proof, and everything else leaves a quiet receipt.

Origin, in my words

"I'm the default tour guide for my friends in New York and wherever we travel. Still, when I travel alone I get anxious about missing an announcement or taking a wrong turn. On the subway I keep seeing elderly riders and people who don't speak English as a first language struggle when an announcement crackles or the plan changes at the last minute. Overheard knows your itinerary and tells you only what matters to you."

Level 3 · how it works

One fingerprint for every stream

Every booking becomes 40 to 54 yes/no questions in French, Dutch and English, tagged operator, place, change or combo, plus one direct "does this hit booking X?" question. For the demo trip that is about 300 questions. Every input, whatever its form, goes through one gemini-decision-1 call that answers all of them at once, and each booking gets its own score.

What to say about it

  • Decision 1 is the fast path; Hydrogen is the accurate one. Transcribe-and-judge made no mistakes on rehearsal data at 7 to 12 times the latency. The full policy caught 18 of 18.
  • Carbon earns its place on recall. It rescued all 3 rehearsal misses and added 2 false alerts.
  • Learning works without hurting real alerts. A real MTA false alert went from 0.79 to 0.01 after one dismissal while the true alert stayed at 0.92. The first version dropped true alerts from 17 to 6; the gated fix keeps them at 17 to 17 and 15 to 14 over two runs.
  • Blurry signs are where it wins on the subway. At 8 px blur the full policy caught 7 of 8 signs against 4 of 8 for both text baselines.

Honest failures

  • near tie A Paris line 8 closure alerted on the Eurostar (0.845) instead of the Paris hotel (0.843).
  • number only "KL 643 gate change; KL 641 departs on time" alerted in every run (0.80 to 0.89). One "not about me" silences it.
  • small sets Only 3 relevant real-news pairs, and a couple of items move between runs on sets this small.
  • latency Verdict about 1 s, translated alert about 10 s, whisper about 18 s, circled photo 25 to 35 s.
  • synthetic media Rehearsal audio and signs are generated, so they are cleaner than a real platform. In heavy subway noise Decision 1 alone fell to 4 of 11 at −10 dB, and the full policy kept up only by sending most pairs to Carbon.
Level 6 · next

Where it goes from here

  • Native app with background listening. Today you tap to listen. A native app could keep the mic in a low-power mode and wake Overheard on speech.
  • Real-time voice with the Live API. Ask a follow-up out loud ("which platform now?") and hear the answer while you walk.
  • More countries and languages. The fingerprint is already multilingual; adding a country is mostly radar sources and rehearsal data.
  • Learning across travellers. A "not about me" from one rider on the 9317 could sharpen the questions for everyone booked on the 9315.
  • Accessibility first. Large-type cards and slower whispers for elderly riders, and the circled photo read aloud for low-vision travellers.
Judge questions

Likely questions, honest answers

You tapped "Not about me". Why not replay the clip?

Because in rehearsal it still alerted. That clip also contains a real gate change for KL 643, so the learned "routine status only" questions answered no. The measured win is on the eval: a real MTA false alert went from 0.79 to 0.01 after one dismissal, with the true alert still at 0.92. Making learning handle mixed announcements is open work.

Why not just use Google Translate?

Translate turns every announcement into words, and you still have to read all of them and decide. Overheard makes the decision for your bookings and stays quiet the rest of the time. Translation is one step that runs after the decision.

Why not TripIt or the airline apps?

They know your bookings and push alerts from operator feeds. They never hear the platform PA, read a paper sign on a pillar, or read Dutch local news about a tram diversion by your hotel. Overheard covers those streams with one engine.

Why Decision 1 instead of a chat model?

One call answers about 300 yes/no questions with probabilities, at 0.56 to 0.93 s median against 4.1 to 6.7 s for asking Hydrogen. Probabilities let us route by threshold. To be fair, Hydrogen in text was more accurate on rehearsal data, which is why the borderline band goes to Carbon.

How do you control false alerts?

The score multiplies "is this my operator or place" by "is this a change", per booking, and news outside a booking's dates scores 0. Then only 0.8 or more alerts, and Carbon reviews 0.4 to 0.8. Full policy: 2 of 12 false on rehearsal audio, 1 of 6 on signs, 6 of 35 on real news. Every Decision 1 false alert on news was a borderline case: right place and dates, wrong change.

How fast is it end to end?

The verdict lands in about 1 s, the translated alert in about 10 s, the whisper by about 18 s, and the circled photo by 25 to 35 s. Shrinking inputs to 16 kHz mono audio and 1024 px images cut Decision 1 from 3.4 s to 0.8 s.

What about Gmail privacy?

The scope is gmail.readonly, the demo uses a lab inbox with 6 sample booking emails, and the OAuth client lives in a gitignored secrets/ folder. Email text does go to the Gemini API for extraction. A product version would read only booking senders and let you paste instead.

What is real and what is synthetic?

Real: the 38 radar news items found today, the live MTA feed (33 alerts), real platform noise and 4 real signs in the subway eval, and the live Gmail read. Synthetic: the rehearsal announcements and signs (Gemini TTS and Nano Banana, labelled by construction) and the 6 bookings, which are fixtures sent as emails.

What broke?

A near tie sent a Paris metro closure to the Eurostar booking (0.845 vs 0.843). Number-only near misses like KL 643 vs KL 641 alert every run. The first learning version dropped true alerts from 17 to 6 until we gated it. One Neon ingest call hung past 3 minutes, so it now times out at 30 s with a Hydrogen fallback.

Why not transcribe and ask an LLM every time?

On clean data that is just as accurate and 7 to 12 times slower. On blurry signs, scoring the image directly wins: 7 of 8 at 8 px blur against 4 of 8 for the text baselines. On heavy noise, transcription wins, and that is the next routing change.

Isn't the 0.3 / 0.7 weighting tuned on your own test set?

Yes. It was picked on the small subway eval set, and the README says so. Treat the perfect clean subway scores as an upper bound.

How did one person build this in a day?

The subway engine came first. Then an orchestrator session split the trip-abroad version into 8 tickets run as parallel Claude Code sessions, and Gemini CLI wrote the sample emails (20 s) and all 36 rehearsal scripts (56 s), and Carbon reviewed the news labels (38 of 38). 71 tests pass.

Where is the Managed Agent?

Video frames go through the same Decision 1 call, and the Antigravity Managed Agent cuts the relevant seconds into a captioned clip with ffmpeg in a hosted sandbox, in about 66 s. A local ffmpeg clip shows first so the demo never waits.

How does it handle languages?

Fingerprint questions are written in French, Dutch and English, announcements arrive in any of those, and the alert is translated and whispered in your language. Hindi is the demo default.

Does the mic run all the time? What about battery?

Not today. You tap to listen. Always-on listening needs a native app with on-device speech detection, which is first on the roadmap.

What does it cost to run?

We have not measured cost per trip yet. Decision 1 is the call that runs on every input and it is the cheapest one in the pipeline; the expensive calls (translation, TTS, the circled photo) only run on alerts.

What is next?

A native app, Live API voice follow-ups, more countries, learning across travellers, and accessibility modes for elderly and low-vision riders.
